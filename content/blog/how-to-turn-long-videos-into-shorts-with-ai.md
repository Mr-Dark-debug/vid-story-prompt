To turn a long video into Shorts with AI, give the system a clear editorial brief, generate or search for candidate moments, reject clips that need missing context, repair the boundaries, then check the vertical frame, captions, audio, rights, and platform settings before export. AI is most useful in the discovery and first-cut stages. The publish decision stays with you.

This workflow is for podcasts, interviews, webinars, tutorials, course lessons, product demos, and other authorised recordings that contain several distinct ideas. It is not a reason to repost footage you do not control, and it is not a guarantee that every long video contains a good short.

## 1. Define the short before you open the tool

“Find the best moments” is not an editorial brief. Best for what?

Write four lines before processing the source:

- **Viewer:** who should understand or care about this excerpt?
- **Job:** should it teach one method, answer one question, show proof, entertain, or lead into the full video?
- **Destination:** where will it be published, and what frame or duration constraints apply?
- **Exclusions:** what topics, claims, guests, music, or private details must not appear?

A useful brief might be: “Find complete answers for first-time freelance designers. Each clip should teach one pricing decision without referring to slides that will be outside the crop. Exclude client names.” That gives a selection model and a human reviewer something concrete to test.

If you have several goals, run separate passes. A testimonial, a tutorial, and an episode teaser need different moments even when they come from the same recording.

## 2. Choose a source with idea density

Runtime is a poor predictor of clip yield. A focused 18-minute interview can contain more usable shorts than a two-hour stream with long gaps and repeated points.

Look for a source with:

- distinct questions, examples, demonstrations, or stories;
- clear enough speech for transcription;
- moments that can make sense without the original introduction;
- usable audio and a subject that can survive the intended crop;
- permission for every element you plan to republish.

Vizard's current help documentation makes a similar practical point from the product side: clip output varies with spoken content, source length, duration settings, and suitability, and the system may return no candidates when there is not enough useful material. More source minutes do not automatically create more publishable ideas.

Use the original or another authorised source file where possible. A public video page is not evidence that you can download, edit, or redistribute its contents. Keep source rights separate from platform visibility.

## 3. Generate a broad first pass

Upload the source or connect it through a supported, authorised import. Choose a target duration range, frame, language, and number of candidates if the tool provides those controls. Then add the brief as a topic or selection prompt.

Different products expose different levers. Descript's Create clips tool lets a user choose one to 20 clips, a duration from 10 seconds to five minutes, a layout, and optional criteria. OpusClip documents topic and timeframe controls when a general result is not the wanted moment. The exact interface matters less than the principle: tell the system what relevance means before judging its ranking.

For an unfamiliar source, a broad first pass can show what is present. For a known recording, prompt-led retrieval usually reduces noise. If you remember a strong section around the second guest question, constrain the timeframe instead of paying review attention to the entire episode again.

Do not style every result yet. Candidate generation is a search stage. Captions, brand colors, and transitions make a weak selection more expensive to reject.

## 4. Triage with a context budget

The most common clipping error is not poor visual quality. It is a sentence that sounds strong only because the missing setup did the real work.

Give each candidate a context score:

| Score | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | A new viewer can understand the subject, claim, and payoff from the clip itself | Keep for boundary review |
| 1 | One short title or one preceding sentence would supply the missing setup | Repair selectively |
| 2 | The viewer needs substantial earlier discussion, a missing slide, or knowledge of an unnamed person | Return to the source |

This is a budget, not a writing challenge. Do not cover a score-2 excerpt with a long title card and call it standalone. Find the earlier setup, combine a coherent range if the tool supports it, or choose another moment.

Then apply three more gates:

1. **One payoff:** the clip reaches an answer, reveal, demonstration, or clear turn; it does not merely promise one.
2. **Faithful meaning:** removing the surrounding conversation does not turn a qualification into an absolute claim.
3. **Distinct role:** the candidate adds something the other approved clips do not.

Rejecting duplicates matters. AI systems can return several windows around the same high-scoring passage. Pick the version with the cleanest setup and ending rather than publishing near-identical cuts.

## 5. Repair the boundaries before the hook

Now watch the kept candidate with a few seconds of source footage on both sides. The generated start may land after the subject was named. The generated end may cut before the example, laugh, breath, or visual reaction that makes the thought feel complete.

Set the start at the earliest point that creates clarity without adding dead setup. That can be the question, a rewritten on-screen title, or the speaker's first complete clause. Set the end after the payoff and before the next topic begins.

Run a cold-open test: play the clip without its caption, title, filename, or knowledge of the full episode. If “he,” “that,” “the second one,” or “what happened next” has no clear reference, the start is still too late.

Official Adobe Express instructions explicitly include reviewing candidates, trimming or expanding them, correcting a bad crop, and opening them in an editor. Treat those actions as the normal workflow. “Generated” does not mean “finished.”

## 6. Make the vertical frame tell the truth

Changing a landscape recording to 9:16 is not just resizing. Decide what the viewer must see at each point.

For a single talking head, a stable crop may be enough. For an interview, the active speaker and the listener's reaction may both matter. For a tutorial or product demo, the cursor, screen, hands, diagram, or before-and-after result may carry more information than the speaker's face.

Watch for four failures:

- the crop follows the wrong person;
- a slide or demonstration is cut off;
- rapid automated movement creates visual jitter;
- burned-in source text collides with new captions or platform controls.

If the needed evidence cannot fit the vertical canvas, use a composed layout, manually keyframe the crop, or reject that passage for this destination. Do not imply that every clipping product provides reliable subject tracking or multi-speaker layouts; those capabilities vary sharply.

## 7. Correct captions as editorial text

Automatic captions give you a draft transcript, not a legal record. Check names, brand terms, numbers, negations, technical language, and homophones against the audio. A missing “not” can reverse the claim. A wrong price or date can make an otherwise good clip unusable.

Break lines by meaning rather than forcing every screen to contain the same number of words. Keep the current phrase readable long enough to follow the speech. Avoid covering faces, demonstrations, or interface controls. Then watch once with sound muted: the captions should communicate the point without becoming a second, competing edit.

## 8. Check audio continuity and visual edits together

Boundary changes, silence trimming, and transcript edits can create clicks, clipped consonants, unnatural breaths, or visible jump cuts. Listen on headphones and a phone speaker. Watch the speaker's mouth at every cut. If a pause carries hesitation, humor, or emphasis, removing it may make the clip less clear rather than more efficient.

Music needs its own rights and mix review. A track licensed for the original long video may not be cleared for a separate social excerpt or another platform. If you cannot verify the permission, remove or replace it through an authorised workflow.

## 9. Export for the actual destination

Check current first-party platform guidance on the day you publish. Do not copy a duration table from an old blog post.

As of 2026-07-31, [YouTube's upload guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12779649?hl=en-GB) says a Short uploaded from a computer can be up to three minutes and must use a square or vertical aspect ratio. That is a technical ceiling, not a recommendation to stretch a 34-second idea to three minutes. YouTube also documents additional Content ID consequences for Shorts over one minute, so rights review and duration can interact.

Export a clean master when your workflow allows it. Avoid downloading a published clip with another platform's interface or watermark and using that as the next platform's source. Keep the approved edit, captions, and metadata together so corrections can be made without recreating the cut.

## 10. Run a 60-second pre-publish check

Before scheduling, answer these questions:

- Does the first spoken line identify the subject or create a precise reason to continue?
- Does the ending deliver what the opening set up?
- Would the clip misrepresent the speaker without the surrounding discussion?
- Are names, figures, and captions correct?
- Is every important person or object visible in the frame?
- Are the source, music, guests, and graphics authorised for this use?
- Does the file meet the current platform's upload rules?

If any answer is uncertain, keep the clip as a draft. Fast publishing does not recover the time spent correcting a misleading or rights-sensitive post later.

## A worked example: one webinar, three different shorts

Imagine a 42-minute product webinar with a host, a customer, and a screen demonstration. This is a hypothetical workflow, not a performance claim.

The first AI pass returns nine candidates. Three cover the same customer quote, two begin with “that was the turning point” without naming what changed, one depends on a chart outside the vertical crop, and three contain complete ideas.

The editor keeps one customer quote, one objection-and-answer, and one demonstration. The context budget sends the two vague excerpts back to the source. Extending one candidate by seven seconds restores the question; trimming another removes a repeated sentence. The demonstration uses a manual crop that favors the interface rather than the host. Names and a numerical value are corrected in captions. Only those three move to export.

The useful output is not “nine clips generated.” It is “three different clips approved with known repairs.” That is the number to compare across sources and tools.

## Turn rejection reasons into a better next pass

Keep a short rejection log: missing setup, no payoff, duplicate idea, wrong topic, bad boundary, unusable crop, caption risk, or rights issue. After several sources, the pattern tells you what to change.

If most candidates lack setup, ask for complete answers that include the question. If they repeat one theme, request named topics in separate passes. If discovery is strong but framing is consistently expensive, keep the selection tool and change the finishing workflow instead of replacing everything.
