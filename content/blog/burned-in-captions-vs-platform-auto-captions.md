Use a **corrected selectable caption track** for long-lived video when the player supports it. Use **burned-in captions** for short-form feeds or creative treatments where you need the text to appear regardless of player controls. Do not rely on unreviewed platform auto-captions for names, numbers, jargon, or sensitive claims.

The strongest production setup is often not one format or the other. Keep a clean video master and a corrected timed-text file, then make a separate burned-in social export when the destination justifies it.

## The terms are easy to mix up

**Burned-in captions** are rendered into the video pixels. They are also called open captions, hardcoded captions, or baked-in subtitles. Every viewer sees the same text in the same position. The player cannot turn it off.

**Closed or selectable captions** live in a separate timed-text track. A viewer uses the CC control to show or hide them. Depending on the player, the viewer may change their size, color, background, or language.

**Platform auto-captions** are one kind of selectable caption track generated by the platform's speech-recognition system after upload. Their existence does not mean a human checked the words or timing.

**Uploaded captions** are also selectable, but the creator supplies the corrected SRT, VTT, or another supported file. This is the option most comparisons overlook. The real choice is not “stylish burned text versus bad machine text.” You can upload a reviewed closed-caption track.

## Comparison at a glance

| Decision | Burned-in captions | Corrected selectable track | Unreviewed platform auto-captions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Always visible | Yes | No | No |
| Viewer can hide text | No | Yes | Yes |
| Viewer can customize appearance | No | Often | Often |
| Creator controls exact style | Yes | Limited by player/file format | Usually limited |
| Correct without re-rendering video | No | Yes | Usually, inside platform editor |
| Multiple languages | Requires separate video renders | Multiple tracks can share one video | Platform-dependent |
| Search/transcript features | Text is only pixels | Player can use timed text | Platform can use its track |
| Survives download/repost | Yes | Only if the track remains attached or is copied | Usually remains on the original platform only |
| Main risk | Permanent errors, blocked picture, no viewer control | Platform support and separate-file management | Recognition errors and missing sound cues |

## Why selectable captions are the better master

Selectable captions preserve viewer choice. Someone who does not need text can hide it. Someone who needs larger type or a stronger background may be able to customize the player. Vimeo OTT, for example, lets viewers change font size, color, edge, background, and opacity for soft captions. Burned text fixes all those decisions at export.

A separate track is also easier to maintain. If a guest's title changes or a name was misspelled, replace or edit the caption file rather than rendering and redistributing the video. YouTube Studio currently lets creators edit automatic caption text and timing, publish a corrected track, or download and upload caption files. Vimeo supports SRT and WebVTT, and Facebook documents SRT upload for video captions.

Language handling is more efficient. One clean video can carry several caption or subtitle tracks. Burned-in language variants require a separate render for each language and a reliable way to keep the filenames and thumbnails distinct.

Timed text can power more than the CC overlay. W3C notes that caption files and transcripts can help search engines index content. Vimeo's transcript view uses caption data for searchable navigation. Text burned into pixels cannot provide that function without a second transcription process.

Most importantly, captions are an access feature, not merely a visual hook. W3C says automatic captions often need editing. A CC button attached to an inaccurate track does not complete the work.

## Why burned-in captions still earn a social variant

Short-form feeds do not expose caption controls consistently across every surface, repost, embed, or downloaded file. Burned-in text travels with the picture. It appears during silent autoplay and survives when an approved clip is uploaded to another service without its original text track.

The creator also controls layout. A burned layer can avoid platform buttons, match a brand, highlight a key term, and respond to the composition. That control is useful when the caption is part of the pacing of a vertical edit.

There are real costs:

- viewers cannot switch it off or restyle it;
- one placement may cover a face, demonstration, or platform interface;
- a typo requires a new render and re-upload;
- translation needs another rendered video;
- aggressive word animation can make reading harder;
- the text consumes picture space on every device;
- optical character recognition may be needed to recover the words later.

Keep the burned treatment restrained enough to function as captions. A sequence of isolated words, emojis, and effects may decorate speech without giving a deaf viewer a coherent account of it. Include meaningful non-speech audio when it affects understanding.

## Why platform auto-captions need a review

Platform auto-captions save the transcription and rough-timing step. They are valuable as a draft and as a fallback when a creator has supplied nothing. They are not a factual source.

YouTube warns that automatic captions can misrepresent speech because of accents, dialects, pronunciation, or background noise, and it asks creators to review and correct them. Its documentation also lists overlapping speakers and simultaneous languages as failure conditions. Names, acronyms, prices, medical terms, and negative words deserve special attention because one substitution can change the claim.

Review these dimensions separately:

1. **Accuracy:** Do the words, names, numbers, and sound cues match?
2. **Synchronicity:** Do cues appear and disappear with the sound?
3. **Completeness:** Is every necessary spoken and non-spoken part included?
4. **Placement:** Does text avoid faces and essential on-screen information?

That four-part model is a useful editorial checklist across formats: it tests whether viewers receive the same information at the moment it matters without the captions obscuring the picture.

## Choose by distribution scenario

### Long-form YouTube, education, webinars, and archives

Upload a clean master plus a corrected caption track. YouTube supports common formats including plain UTF-8 SRT and WebVTT, though style support varies. Keep the transcript for revision and additional languages. Add burned text only when the visual design truly requires it.

### TikTok, Reels, Shorts, and feed-first clips

Create a reviewed burned-in variant when you need guaranteed visible text and consistent placement. Test the actual upload surface for buttons and duplicate auto-captions. Keep the clean master and timed transcript so another destination does not inherit a permanent platform-specific layout.

### Owned website or product player

Prefer a proper selectable track and accessible player controls. Do not substitute a transcript below the video for synchronized captions; the two help different tasks. Offer a transcript as an additional navigation and reference tool.

### Paid social ads

Burned-in text can ensure the intended message appears in silent playback, but keep the copy concise and inside the ad placement's safe area. Preserve a clean master for localization and creative tests. Confirm the destination's current accessibility and caption options rather than assuming an organic-post workflow applies to ads.

### Internal review or client approval

Use a clean file with a selectable corrected track when reviewers need to inspect text or replace terminology. A burned review copy can be useful for placement approval, but it should not become the only master.

## A two-master workflow without duplicate work

You do not need to transcribe the same video twice.

1. Lock the final video and audio edit.
2. Generate one timed transcript with an [AI caption workflow](/blog/add-automatic-captions-to-videos-with-ai).
3. Correct names, wording, sound cues, speaker changes, timing, and line breaks.
4. Save the corrected timed text as the source of truth.
5. Export a clean video master.
6. Upload the timed-text track to players that support it.
7. Render a separate burned-in social version from the same corrected text.
8. Test each destination with captions on and off.

If the program was edited from a transcript, generate or re-align captions after the cut is locked. The [transcript podcast editing workflow](/blog/podcast-editing-with-transcripts) explains why the source transcript should not automatically become the final timed caption deliverable.

Name the assets so nobody posts the wrong version:

- `episode-12-clean-master.mp4`
- `episode-12-en-reviewed.vtt`
- `episode-12-social-en-burned.mp4`

## Avoid the double-caption failure

The most common combined approach is also the ugliest when unmanaged: burned-in text sits near the center while the platform displays a second automatic track at the bottom. The words may disagree or arrive at different times.

Before publishing:

- upload a private or unlisted test where possible;
- play once with the platform CC setting on and once off;
- check the opening and every layout change at phone size;
- confirm which caption track is marked as the language default;
- remove or disable an unreviewed duplicate track when the platform permits;
- retain the corrected closed track even if the social export also contains burned text.

Do not solve duplication by deleting the only accessible track from a long-form player. Use the clean master there and reserve the burned variant for the surfaces that need it.

## The decision rule

Choose a corrected selectable track when viewer control, accessibility, languages, maintenance, and search matter. Choose burned-in captions when the distribution surface cannot be trusted to show a track and exact visual placement is necessary. Treat platform automatic captions as a draft to review or replace.

If you only have time for one long-lived master, keep the picture clean and the corrected timed text separate. Pixels are easy to add in a social render. They are expensive to remove after publication.
