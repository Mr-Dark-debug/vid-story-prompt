To turn a landscape YouTube video into a 9:16 Short, create a vertical timeline, decide what the viewer must see in every shot, then crop, reposition, or change layouts around that evidence. Do not apply one centre crop to the whole source and assume the job is done. A 9:16 file can pass the format check while hiding the second speaker, the cursor, the product, or the punchline.

Automatic reframing can generate a useful motion path. It cannot decide which visual detail proves the spoken claim. Treat it as a first edit that needs review.

## Start with the geometry

A 16:9 frame is wide; a 9:16 frame is tall. If a 1920×1080 source is enlarged to fill a 1080×1920 vertical canvas, most of the source width falls outside the crop. The editor is not rearranging the full image into a new shape. It is choosing a narrow moving window through it.

There are three basic ways to place landscape footage on a vertical canvas:

| Treatment | What happens | Best for | Main cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fill and crop | Video fills the vertical canvas; left and right are removed | One clear subject, close framing | Wide context and edge details disappear |
| Fit full frame | Whole 16:9 frame remains visible | Slides, screens, groups, comparisons | Image becomes smaller; space remains above and below |
| Recompose | Crop, cuts, stacked regions, or overlays change by shot | Interviews, tutorials, gameplay, demonstrations | More editorial work and more points to check |

Never stretch the 16:9 image to 9:16. Stretching keeps every pixel on screen by distorting faces, circles, text, and movement. Scale proportionally and accept either a crop or a designed layout.

YouTube says its player adapts to the uploaded video's aspect ratio and advises against adding padding or black bars directly to the file for ordinary uploads. For a deliberate vertical composition, however, preserving a landscape frame within the 9:16 canvas can be the honest editorial choice when every part of the frame matters. Use the remaining space as part of the design rather than pretending the crop is full-screen footage.

## Make an evidence map before touching the crop

Watch the candidate clip once and write down every visual element the viewer needs to understand the words. This is an evidence map.

For a software tutorial, the map might be:

- 00:00–00:03: incorrect caption line and timing marker;
- 00:03–00:08: cursor opens the subtitle settings on the right;
- 00:08–00:14: one control changes;
- 00:14–00:20: corrected playback and waveform.

A centred crop may work for the first and last shots but fail when the cursor moves to a side panel. The map tells you where the framing treatment must change. It also reveals candidates that should not become Shorts: if a data table needs its full width and cannot remain legible at phone size, choose a different moment or rebuild the visual rather than publishing an unreadable miniature.

For an interview, record who speaks, whose reaction matters, and whether both faces need to remain visible. For sports or demonstrations, include the object being tracked—not just the person. For a slide presentation, identify the exact label or chart region referred to by the narration.

## Pick one of five treatments for each shot

### 1. Fixed crop for a stable subject

Use a static 9:16 crop when one speaker or object stays within a predictable area. Set the crop once, leave comfortable headroom, and check hands or props that enter near the edges.

This is often better than motion tracking for a seated talking head. A crop that drifts after every small gesture can feel less intentional than a steady frame. Adobe's current Auto Reframe documentation includes a slower-motion preset for footage with little camera movement, reinforcing the point: not every shot needs frequent keyframes.

### 2. Keyed pan for a moving subject

If the important subject crosses the original frame, animate the horizontal position. Place keyframes around meaningful moves, then preview the speed. The crop should arrive before the action or pointer becomes important, not chase it after it leaves the frame.

Avoid constant micro-corrections. They create a nervous camera that was not present in the source. Cut the movement into segments when direction changes sharply.

### 3. Editorial cut between points of interest

When two speakers alternate, cutting from one stable crop to the other is often clearer than sliding the frame across the room. Preserve a two-shot for interruptions, laughter, or exchanges where the reaction carries meaning.

The audio cut and visual cut do not always need the same boundary. Let the listener hear the end of the question while the picture changes to the respondent, but avoid switching so early that the viewer cannot identify the first speaker.

### 4. Split or stacked layout for simultaneous evidence

Use a stacked layout when the viewer needs a speaker and a screen, gameplay, product demo, or second feed at the same time. Allocate space according to the task. A tiny screen recording under a large face is wrong if the viewer needs to read the UI.

This approach solves a different problem from tracking. Tracking chooses where one crop points. A split layout preserves multiple sources or regions. Current OpusClip documentation, for example, describes speaker/gameplay proportions and screenshare-aware layouts; regardless of tool, check that text remains legible and the crop does not repeat the same empty area.

### 5. Preserve the full frame when context wins

Place the complete landscape image inside the vertical canvas when cropping would remove essential context. Use a solid, branded, or restrained blurred background in the unused space. Captions can occupy some of that space, but do not make the original picture so small that faces and text become decorative thumbnails.

This treatment is useful for group shots, before-and-after comparisons, wide choreography, diagrams, and some screen recordings. It fills less of the display, yet showing the whole fact is better than presenting a dramatic crop that no longer explains the audio.

## Run auto reframe as a draft

In editors that support automatic reframing, the broad workflow is:

1. Duplicate the finished landscape sequence so the source edit remains intact.
2. Set a vertical 9:16 target, commonly 1080×1920 for a Full HD export.
3. Run the reframing analysis on the selected sequence or clips.
4. Review every cut, speaker change, fast move, screen change, and graphic.
5. Replace tracking with fixed crops, manual keyframes, or layouts where needed.
6. Add or reposition final captions and graphics.
7. Export and watch the complete file at phone size.

Adobe says Premiere's Auto Reframe identifies action, creates a duplicate sequence, and offers motion presets. The same documentation warns that complex sequences with rapid movement or multiple points of interest may need keyframe refinement. That warning applies beyond Premiere: automated tracking follows a detected subject; it does not know that the quiet listener's reaction or an edge label is the point of the clip.

Inspect these failure points closely:

- a crop jumps when another face enters;
- a hand, product, ball, or cursor leaves the frame;
- a speaker turns away and the tracker chooses the background;
- the crop changes during a dissolve or graphic transition;
- captions cover the newly centred subject;
- a lower-third from the landscape edit becomes half-visible;
- the source camera pans while the crop pans in the opposite direction.

If the automated path needs a correction every second, stop repairing it. Split the shot into stable sections or choose a layout that preserves the full area.

## Reframe common source types differently

### One-person talking head

Start with a fixed crop. Keep the eyes comfortably below the top edge, preserve gestures that support the explanation, and leave room for captions. Use a keyed move only when the speaker changes position enough to leave the composition.

### Two-person podcast

Map speaker changes before choosing the layout. Cut between individual crops for clean turns; use the two-shot for overlaps and reactions. A tool must identify both who is speaking and how the faces are positioned. Those are separate problems, which is why a transcript-perfect edit can still frame the wrong person.

### Screen recording or slide deck

Do not follow the narrator's face while the instructions refer to a control. Crop to the active UI region, zoom only until text remains sharp, and cut to a new region when the task moves. Rebuild essential labels as native text if they cannot survive the crop. A full-frame fit may be better for diagrams with relationships across the entire slide.

### Gameplay, sport, or demonstration

Track the evidence chain: player plus ball, hands plus object, presenter plus result. The most visually prominent person is not always the subject. Use fewer, purposeful moves and keep scoreboards or timers visible only when the narration depends on them.

## Place captions after the composition is stable

Generate and correct the transcript early, but finalise caption placement after reframing. A caption designed for the lower third of a landscape edit can land over a face, control, or platform interface in the vertical version.

Use a phone-sized preview and inspect the whole clip rather than one clean frame. Captions may be safe while the subject is centred and collide when a crop pans. Keep line lengths readable, avoid edge-hugging text, and test the upload with YouTube's own interface visible.

The [YouTube Shorts size and aspect-ratio guide](/blog/youtube-shorts-size-length-resolution-aspect-ratio) separates current platform rules from production recommendations. In short: YouTube accepts square or vertical Shorts under its current duration conditions; 1080×1920 is a sensible 9:16 working target, not a magic quality upgrade. A low-resolution or heavily compressed source does not gain detail because its export canvas is larger.

## Quality-control the vertical export

Before uploading, watch once for story and once only for framing. Check:

- every named person, object, control, chart label, and reaction is visible when referenced;
- the crop does not jump without an editorial reason;
- faces and graphics are not cut at uncomfortable edges;
- no source text has become too small to read;
- captions stay clear of subjects and likely interface overlays;
- the image is scaled proportionally, with no stretching;
- the file has no accidental baked padding;
- the first frame still supports the [Shorts hook](/blog/how-to-write-youtube-shorts-hooks);
- the export retains the source frame rate and sufficient quality for the chosen canvas.
